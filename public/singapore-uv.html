Singapore's ultraviolet day, hour by hour

What the national measurements show about when the sun here is actually dangerous, and what that means for skin at this latitude.

7 September 20261,856 words18 references

Singapore lies roughly one and a half degrees north of the equator, its day length varies by about an hour across the entire year16, and the National Environment Agency reports that it is common for the ultraviolet index to reach Very High and Extreme levels in the four-hour period between 11am and 3pm on a day with little cloud cover8. Yet fewer than a quarter of adults surveyed here apply sunscreen2. This article sets out what is measured, when, and what follows from it for skin.

What the ultraviolet index measures

The ultraviolet index is a standardised scale developed jointly by the World Health Organization, the United Nations Environment Programme and the World Meteorological Organization to express the intensity of ultraviolet radiation at ground level8. The scale is weighted for erythema, calibrated to the wavelengths that redden human skin rather than to total ultraviolet energy.

The index is measured here with a sensor sited in an open field at the Changi Meteorological Station, and reported every fifteen minutes between 7am and 7pm, each value representing the average of the preceding fifteen minutes78. The scale runs from 0 to 11 and above, grouped into five categories. Readings above 11 are considered Extreme, and those between 8 and 10 Very High1. World Health Organization guidance attaches protective behaviour to those categories: at 3 to 7, seek shade during midday hours and use a shirt, sunscreen and hat; at 8 and above, avoid being outside during midday hours altogether14.

“A high UV Index reading means a larger dose of UV radiation for the same amount of time spent in the sun.”

Dr Koh Hong Yi, Dermatology Consultant, Singapore General Hospital13

What the sky here delivers on an ordinary day

The Meteorological Service Singapore gives the typical daily maximum ultraviolet index as six to nine1, which places an ordinary cloudless afternoon in the High to Very High categories before any unusual conditions are involved. In every month the protective threshold of 3 is routinely exceeded around the middle of the day.

What varies through the year is not solar geometry but the atmosphere in front of it. Day length runs to roughly twelve hours and differs by about an hour between the longest and shortest days16, and the sun is close to overhead in every month. Annual variation is therefore driven mainly by cloud, with the highest values occurring in months with less of it: February, March, April and September tend to see the highest ultraviolet radiation1.

The National Environment Agency publishes its hourly index readings as an open dataset. The 2024 record alone contains more than 32,000 hourly observations from the Changi station6, which is the resource anyone wanting to characterise the local ultraviolet day in detail should be working from.

One measured afternoon, hour by hour

The clearest way to see the shape of the day is to follow a single one. On 27 March 2024 the index at Changi first entered the Extreme band at about 12.15pm with a reading of 11. It climbed to 12 at about 1.15pm. By 2.45pm it had fallen back to 101.

Three points, roughly two and a half hours apart, describe the whole problem. The reading was already in the highest category before the lunch hour was over, it peaked in the early afternoon rather than at midday, and it was still Very High at a quarter to three. An office worker leaving the building at 12.30pm and returning at 2pm was outdoors for the entire span.

Why the clock misleads you

Singapore keeps time eight hours ahead of Coordinated Universal Time, but sits at a longitude nearer to seven. The consequence is that solar noon, the moment the sun reaches its highest point, falls at around 1pm rather than at midday: in January it occurs at approximately 1.13pm, with sunrise at 7.11am and sunset at 7.16pm16.

This is why the 27 March peak arrived when it did. The four-hour window of highest intensity is not symmetrical around twelve o'clock, and the intuition that danger peaks at lunchtime and recedes through the early afternoon is displaced by roughly an hour. The 11am to 3pm window published by the National Environment Agency8 accommodates this, but the underlying reason is rarely explained, and a 2.30pm walk is frequently treated as safer than it is.

What reaches the skin

Terrestrial ultraviolet radiation is conventionally divided into ultraviolet B, spanning approximately 290 to 320 nanometres, and ultraviolet A, spanning 320 to 400 nanometres3. The proportions are heavily skewed. Ultraviolet B accounts for around 0.5 per cent of solar spectral irradiance at ground level, while ultraviolet A represents approximately 95 per cent of the ultraviolet radiation reaching the surface and is at least twenty times more abundant315.

The two behave differently in tissue. Of the ultraviolet B reaching the skin, around 70 per cent is absorbed by the stratum corneum, 20 per cent reaches the viable epidermis and only 10 per cent penetrates the upper dermis. Ultraviolet A travels further, with 20 to 30 per cent reaching the deep dermis. Ultraviolet B is the more effective waveband for producing erythema by roughly three orders of magnitude, which is why the index is weighted towards it; ultraviolet A appears to play the major role in the deterioration of dermal structure that produces the photoaged appearance3.

Sunburn is a signal generated almost entirely by the minority waveband. The waveband responsible for most dermal change produces no immediate sensation at all, is present in far greater quantity throughout the reporting day, and passes through window glass.

What cloud and reflection do and do not change

Cloud cover is the most common source of false reassurance. The World Health Organization states that light or thin cloud has little effect on ultraviolet levels and may even enhance them through scattering15. In a climate with substantial convective cloud but frequent breaks, this produces a pattern in which the index falls sharply during a thunderstorm and returns to Very High shortly afterwards, while the subjective impression is of an overcast day.

Reflected radiation adds to direct exposure and is not blocked by conventional shade. Dry sand reflects approximately 15 per cent of incident ultraviolet radiation and sea foam approximately 25 per cent15. A parasol on a beach, or an umbrella on a bright pavement, reduces the direct component while leaving the scattered and reflected components largely intact.

Shade nonetheless remains the single most effective measure available, and the index-linked guidance is explicit that it should be sought from a value of 3 upwards and that outdoor exposure should be avoided from 814. On an ordinary afternoon sitting between 6 and 91, that is not an occasional instruction.

What this does to skin here, and what people do

The clinical picture at this latitude is dominated by pigment rather than by cancer. Analysis of 12,692 skin cancers recorded by the Singapore Cancer Registry between 1968 and 2016 found melanoma accounted for 5.8 per cent of cases, with melanoma and squamous cell carcinoma incidence remaining low and stable across the Chinese, Malay and Indian populations; basal cell carcinoma was the subtype that rose11. Pigmented changes, meanwhile, constitute the major sign of skin photoageing in Asian populations3, and melasma, the symmetrical facial hyperpigmentation that predominates in Fitzpatrick skin types III and IV, is both common and difficult to treat: a randomised trial conducted at the National Skin Centre required daily SPF 50 or higher in every arm as baseline care4.

Against that, local behaviour is poorly matched to local exposure. A cross-sectional survey of 2,328 adults found that 14.2 per cent wore protective headgear, 18.8 per cent used umbrellas regularly and 23.9 per cent applied a sunscreen of SPF 30 or above. Mean daily sun exposure was 1.45 hours. Just over half the sample, 53.4 per cent, had Fitzpatrick type III skin and a further 19.1 per cent type IV, which is precisely the group in which melasma predominates. Men, those with darker skin types and those with lower education spent more time in the sun and scored lower on protection2.

“Although [having dark skin] can have a certain protective effect, if they do not wear sunscreen and are exposed to the sun for long periods of time, they will have a higher chance of suffering from skin cancer.”

Dr Yew Yik Weng, National Skin Centre, co-author of the survey12

What adequate protection looks like, and what the evidence supports

Sun protection factor is determined under laboratory conditions at an application density of 2 milligrams per square centimetre5. Application in ordinary use is generally well below that density, and because the relationship between quantity and protection is not linear, the protection achieved is materially lower than the labelled figure. Reapplication through a day of 6 to 9 is what makes the labelled figure approximately true.

The guidance issued locally is for a broad-spectrum product of at least SPF 30, reapplied approximately every two hours, together with umbrellas, broad-brimmed hats and sunglasses1. Ultraviolet A dominates the incident spectrum and passes through glass, so a product's ultraviolet A performance matters as much as its sun protection factor. Where a patient has recently undergone a light or laser procedure, daily photoprotection is recommended from the first day afterwards and maintained for three to six months, with visible-light protection where pigmentary relapse is the concern17.

Formulation choice is also a compliance question. Heat and humidity raise sebum production and sweat rate, and a product that is not tolerable to wear in this climate is not applied, which makes texture a clinical variable rather than a cosmetic preference. Where reapplication over make-up during the working day is the obstacle, powder and stick formats exist precisely to remove it.

The underlying benefit has been tested. A randomised controlled trial assigned 1,621 adults to daily or discretionary sunscreen use and followed them for well over a decade; invasive melanoma occurred in three participants in the daily-use group against eleven in the discretionary group, a hazard ratio of 0.27 with a confidence interval of 0.08 to 0.979. In the same cohort, 903 adults under 55 followed for four and a half years showed 24 per cent less skin ageing on blinded assessment when assigned to daily use10. The most frequently raised objection does not survive examination either: a review of experimental studies, field trials and 69 observational studies concluded that there is little evidence that sunscreen decreases 25-hydroxyvitamin D concentration in real-life settings, although the authors note that the field trials used sunscreen of around SPF 1618.

The index sits in the High to Very High range on an ordinary afternoon, there is no month in which that stops being true, and on 27 March 2024 it was still at 10 at a quarter to three. Protection here is a daily variable, and the four hours between 11am and 3pm are the ones that determine most of the annual dose.

Frequently asked questions

What is a normal ultraviolet index for Singapore?

The Meteorological Service Singapore gives the typical daily maximum as six to nine, which corresponds to the High and Very High categories. On 27 March 2024 the reading at Changi reached 121. The National Environment Agency reports that Very High and Extreme levels are common between 11am and 3pm on a day with little cloud cover8.

Which hours matter most?

The four hours from 11am to 3pm8. Because Singapore's clock runs about an hour ahead of its solar time, solar noon falls at around 1.13pm rather than at midday16, which is why the peak reading on 27 March 2024 was recorded at about 1.15pm1.

Which months are worst?

February, March, April and September tend to see the highest ultraviolet radiation, because annual variation here is driven by cloud cover rather than by the sun's elevation1.

Does an overcast day mean I can skip sunscreen?

No. The World Health Organization states that light or thin cloud has little effect on ultraviolet levels and may even enhance them through scattering15. In a climate of frequent short storms and rapid clearing, the subjective impression of an overcast day is a poor guide to the measured index.

Is sunscreen still necessary indoors or in a car?

Ultraviolet A accounts for approximately 95 per cent of the ultraviolet radiation reaching ground level315, penetrates to the deep dermis3, and passes through window glass. For anyone managing pigmentation or photoageing, daily use is the relevant standard rather than outdoor use.

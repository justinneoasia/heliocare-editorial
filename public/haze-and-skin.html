Photoprotection · Air quality

What the haze is doing to skin

Particulate matter is the insult and ultraviolet is the multiplier. Read against the studies, the case for changing anything is narrower than this week's advice suggests.

9 September 20261,821 words14 references

At 11am on 9 September 2026 the twenty-four hour Pollutant Standards Index across Singapore's five regions read between 72 and 931. Days earlier it had crossed 100 for the first time since October 20232. The skincare advice circulating in response has been confident and almost entirely unsourced. What the published evidence supports is narrower, and it runs out sooner than the advice does.

What is in the air this week

The National Environment Agency attributes this episode to smoke plumes from fires in southern and central Sumatra and parts of Kalimantan, carried over Singapore on southeasterly and southerly winds3. On 4 September the one-hour PM2.5 concentration measured across the island ranged from 41 to 83 micrograms per cubic metre, and the twenty-four hour PSI from 83 to 1163. By 7 September those had eased to 22 to 36 and 63 to 85 respectively, with the agency still forecasting a moderate to low unhealthy range while dry conditions persist4.

For skin the number that matters is not the PSI but PM2.5, the fraction of airborne particulate below 2.5 micrometres in aerodynamic diameter, which the mechanistic literature attributes chiefly to open fires, vehicle exhaust and power generation5 and which in this episode is coming from burning in Sumatra and Kalimantan4. The PSI is a composite across several pollutants, but in a haze episode PM2.5 is the sub-index driving it: in the 9 September reading, the twenty-four hour PSI in all five regions was identical to that region's PM2.5 sub-index1. At 3pm on 8 September the one-hour PM2.5 reading in central Singapore was 58 micrograms per cubic metre6.

The skin evidence is mostly about pigment

The best-known work on particulate matter and skin is not about haze at all, but a follow-up of the German SALIA cohort, in which 400 women aged 70 to 80 were examined against decades of residential exposure to traffic soot and background particulate7. Each increase in soot exposure was associated with roughly 20% more pigment spots on the forehead and cheeks, and each increase in traffic-derived particles with 16 to 17% more7. Nasolabial folds deepened slightly. Wrinkles, notably, showed no significant association anywhere on the face.

A Chinese cohort of 1,877 adults found the same signal indoors: an interquartile rise in modelled indoor PM2.5 was associated with 12.5% more pigment spots on the forehead and 7.7% more upper-lip wrinkling8. Two independent populations and two different exposure routes produced the same finding. That consistency is why the pigmentation claim is the sturdiest thing in this literature.

That sturdiness comes with a limit. Both studies measure exposure accumulated across decades of ordinary urban living, and neither tells you what one week of haze does to a face. Anyone extrapolating from a lifetime of soot to a fortnight of smoke is doing something the data does not license.

The Singapore study, and what it puts a number on

One piece of local evidence speaks directly to short-term exposure. Researchers at the National Skin Centre analysed 1,440,844 atopic dermatitis consultations recorded between 2009 and 2019 against daily air quality, using distributed lag models out to six weeks9. At the 90th percentile of PM2.5, 24.4 micrograms per cubic metre, the risk of an eczema consultation was 10% higher than at the median of 16.1, with a confidence interval of 1.01 to 1.199. At the 10th percentile it was 14% lower. Nitrogen dioxide, sulphur dioxide, ozone and carbon monoxide showed no significant association at all.

The finding is more modest than it first sounds. The eleven years it covers include the 2013 and 2015 haze episodes, so bad air days are inside the range it modelled rather than outside it. The effect is real, specific to a defined inflammatory condition rather than to skin in general, and amounts to a 10% shift in consultations rather than a transformation. The study establishes that when the particulate load in Singapore rises, more people come in with eczema. That is locally measured, and it is the whole of what has been shown.

On comparing this week's numbers to the study

Setting the 58 micrograms per cubic metre recorded in central Singapore on 8 September6 against the study's 90th percentile of 24.49 makes it look as though the risk has more than doubled. That comparison does not hold. The first is a one-hour reading and the second a twenty-four hour average, so they are not the same quantity, and the dose-response relationship in the study is not linear. The readings this week are elevated. By how much the risk moves is not something the published work can tell you.

Where ultraviolet enters the picture

Particulate matter and ultraviolet radiation are different insults that converge on the same chemistry. The polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons carried on fine particles activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, a transcription factor expressed throughout the skin, which drives cytochrome P450 expression and generates reactive oxygen species5. Those species raise matrix metalloproteinase 1 and 3, which degrade collagen and elastin; they suppress collagen type 1 synthesis; and they reduce the structural proteins that hold the barrier together, including filaggrin, E-cadherin and the cytokeratins5. Ultraviolet A arrives at the same oxidative endpoint by a different route.

Whether the two are merely additive or worse in combination now has direct evidence. A 2025 study exposed primary human melanocytes and skin explants to ultraviolet, to urban particulate, and to both together for four consecutive days10. Only the combination reduced mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP beyond what either did alone, only the combination tipped cells from senescence into apoptosis, and the explants showed epidermal thinning and dermal melanin leakage resembling post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation10. About 30% of cells in the combined arm turned senescent.

The counterweight arrived the same year. A separate group exposed keratinocytes and human skin explants to benzo[a]pyrene, the model hydrocarbon, with and without ultraviolet11. In isolated keratinocytes the response was partially synergistic, as expected. In intact skin explants it was not: oxidative markers barely moved, DNA damage did not exceed ultraviolet alone, and the tissue demonstrated repair capacity the cell monolayers lacked11. Intact human skin defends itself considerably better than a dish of cells does. The synergy is real in the laboratory. How much of it survives in a living face is an open question, and the authors of the melanocyte study list the same caveat themselves: four days of controlled exposure in donor tissue from light-skinned neonates is not a proxy for Singaporean skin under Singaporean conditions10.

The thing most haze advice gets wrong

The intuitive assumption is that a sky full of smoke means less ultraviolet reaching the ground, and that photoprotection therefore matters less this week. The World Health Organization's guide to the solar ultraviolet index says the opposite in plain terms: scattering can increase total ultraviolet levels, and haze in the atmosphere can even increase ultraviolet exposure12. Over 90% of ultraviolet penetrates light cloud12. A dimmer, greyer sky is a poor guide to what reaches skin.

Nobody has measured this properly for Singapore. Thick absorbing smoke at high optical depth does reduce surface ultraviolet; lighter scattering haze can raise the diffuse component. Which effect dominates depends on the aerosol load, its composition and the sun's angle, and no published Singapore measurement resolves it for the conditions of this particular week. None of that supports a claim in either direction. The visual dimming of the sun is not evidence of anything, and treating it as permission to skip sunscreen is an inference resting on nothing.

What antioxidants have and have not shown

The most directly relevant trial is small and recent. Fifteen women with phototypes III and IV were exposed to 100 micrograms per cubic metre of particulate for two hours a day across four consecutive days, with three skin zones each: unexposed, exposed, and exposed after a topical antioxidant serum of ascorbic acid, ferulic acid and tocopherol13. In the exposed untreated zones, type I collagen fell by 30 to 40%, type III by around 60%, the oxidative marker 4-HNE rose by 61% and COX2 by 63%. The treated zones showed 43% less 4-HNE and 57% less COX2 than exposed skin, with filaggrin and involucrin substantially preserved13.

Those are striking numbers. Fifteen participants over four days is a pilot, every endpoint is a molecular marker read from a biopsy rather than a visible clinical outcome anyone would notice in a mirror, and several authors are employed by the cosmetics manufacturer whose formulation was tested13. The direction of the finding is plausible and mechanistically coherent. The magnitude, and whether it translates into anything a person can see, has not been established.

What this article is not claiming

Heliocare 360 D Plus is formulated around a standardised Polypodium leucotomos extract with niacinamide, vitamin D and vitamin E, and the manufacturer's published claims for it concern ultraviolet damage, oxidative neutralisation and photoageing14. No pollution or air quality claim is made for the product, and none is made here. Nothing in the published literature tests oral photoprotection against particulate exposure, so there is no evidence base on which such a claim could rest.

What the evidence supports doing

The gap between what has been demonstrated and what is being recommended online this week is wide. Almost nothing in the literature addresses a discrete haze episode directly. The recommendations below are drawn from what has been shown, with the reasoning attached, and they are deliberately unexciting.

  • Keep the sunscreen exactly as it was. There is no measured basis for assuming ultraviolet has fallen, and the World Health Organization's own guidance points the other way12. Nothing about the haze makes a change to photoprotection rational.
  • Cleanse at the end of the day, and do not escalate it. Particles sit on the skin surface and in follicular openings5, so removing them is sensible. Aggressive cleansing is not: the same literature shows particulate exposure already degrades filaggrin and E-cadherin5, and stripping the barrier further works against you.
  • If you have eczema, treat this as a flare-risk week. This is the one recommendation with local, large-scale evidence behind it9. Keep to your maintenance routine rather than waiting for a flare to start.
  • An antioxidant step is reasonable, on modest expectations. The mechanism holds up and the pilot data point the right way, but the evidence is a fifteen-person industry-funded study reading biopsy markers13. Treat it as promising rather than proven.
  • Follow the agency's advisory for outdoor exertion rather than a skincare rule. The National Environment Agency publishes guidance by population group and updates it daily4. Reducing time outdoors is the only intervention here with a genuine evidence base behind it, and it happens to reduce both exposures at once.

The uncomfortable summary is that a haze week is a good reason to be consistent and a poor reason to buy anything new. The particulate load is doing something measurable to skin, the effect is modest at the exposures Singapore experiences, and the interventions with the strongest evidence behind them are the ones most people already own.

Frequently asked questions

Does haze make ultraviolet damage worse or better?

Neither has been measured for Singapore under these conditions. The World Health Organization notes that atmospheric scattering can increase total ultraviolet levels and that haze can even increase exposure12, while dense absorbing smoke reduces surface ultraviolet. Because the answer depends on aerosol load, composition and sun angle, the practical response is to change nothing about photoprotection rather than to assume a direction.

Should I stop wearing sunscreen during the haze?

No. No measurement establishes that ultraviolet at ground level has fallen, and the visual dimming of the sky is not evidence that it has12. Separately, ultraviolet and particulate exposure converge on the same oxidative pathway5, so removing one of the two protections you have is the wrong direction.

Will a week of haze age my skin?

The published evidence does not answer that. The studies linking particulate exposure to pigment spots and skin ageing measure exposure accumulated over decades of urban living, in cohorts of 400 and 1,877 people78. Extrapolating from a lifetime of soot to one hazy fortnight is not something those studies support.

Why does my eczema get worse when the haze comes?

That association is the best-evidenced local effect. An analysis of 1,440,844 atopic dermatitis consultations at the National Skin Centre between 2009 and 2019 found a 10% higher consultation risk at the 90th percentile of PM2.5 compared with the median, with a confidence interval of 1.01 to 1.199. Particulate exposure also reduces barrier proteins including filaggrin and E-cadherin5, which is a plausible mechanism for the association.

Does an oral supplement help against haze?

No published study has tested oral photoprotection against particulate exposure. The manufacturer's claims for Heliocare 360 D Plus concern ultraviolet damage and photoageing, not pollution14, and this article makes no claim beyond that. The topical antioxidant evidence that does exist is a fifteen-person, four-day, industry-funded pilot reading biopsy markers rather than visible outcomes13.

Photoprotection · Evidence review

Sun protection you swallow

What oral photoprotection can and cannot do, read against the trials rather than the packaging.

7 September 20261,804 words14 references

A capsule that makes your skin harder to burn sounds like the answer to a country where the ultraviolet index sits between six and nine on an ordinary day.1 The honest version is narrower than the marketing and more interesting than the scepticism. Here is what has actually been measured in people, who funded the measuring, and where the evidence runs out.

What is actually in the capsule

Oral photoprotection, as sold in Singapore and across Europe, almost always means one ingredient: an extract of Polypodium leucotomos, a fern native to Central America. In the Heliocare range the standardised version of this extract is branded Fernblock, and the manufacturer describes it as working through antioxidant activity, support for the skin's DNA repair processes, and protection of the immune cells in the skin that ultraviolet light suppresses.2

The daily capsule format in the Heliocare 360 range pairs the extract with niacinamide, vitamin E and vitamin D, and is taken once a day before sun exposure.3 Doses across the published research vary considerably by indication, from 240 mg daily up to 1,200 mg, and no standard protocol has been established.4 That variation matters, and we will come back to it.

One thing to settle immediately. Extraction method appears to change what the extract does. The authors of the largest photoprotection review state plainly that their findings “cannot be extrapolated to other formulations” of Polypodium leucotomos.5 A generic fern extract bought online is not the same product as a standardised pharmaceutical-grade one, and the research on one does not transfer to the other.

What the trials measured

The most-used endpoint in this literature is the minimal erythema dose, the amount of ultraviolet energy needed to produce a just-visible reddening of the skin. If a supplement raises it, the skin has become measurably harder to burn.

A 2018 systematic review pooled 18 studies of oral Polypodium leucotomos for photoprotection and found that most reported a benefit on this measure, with no serious adverse effects recorded.5 A broader 2025 review identified 21 qualifying studies including 11 randomised controlled trials, spanning photoageing, actinic keratosis, photodermatoses, melasma, vitiligo and atopic dermatitis.4

That sounds substantial until you look at the size of the individual studies. In the 2018 review, sample sizes ranged from five participants to sixty-one.5 These are small, mostly short trials, and several were funded by the manufacturer. The effect appears real and it appears consistent in direction. It is not large, and it has not been demonstrated at the scale that would settle the question.

Where the evidence is strongest

The single most convincing study is not about sunburn at all. In a randomised trial across two Italian centres, 131 patients with photodamaged skin and at least three actinic keratoses, which are the rough precancerous patches that develop on chronically sun-exposed skin, were assigned to one of three regimens for twelve months: SPF 100+ sunscreen alone, sunscreen plus a topical Polypodium leucotomos cream, or sunscreen plus both the topical cream and 240 mg of the oral extract daily.6

Of the 116 who completed, disease progressed in 38% of the sunscreen-only group, 11% of the topical group and 2% of the group taking the capsule as well. Severity scores improved by 7% in the extract groups while worsening by 6% in the controls.6

Two caveats belong in the same breath. A senior author holds financial relationships with the manufacturer, and the investigators themselves called for larger and adequately powered studies before this changes clinical practice. Separately, a planned independent trial of the extract for skin cancer prevention at Massachusetts General Hospital was withdrawn in 2019 for lack of funding, before a single participant was enrolled.7 The question of whether oral photoprotection prevents skin cancer has, in other words, not been asked properly yet.

The melasma question, and the Singapore trial

Melasma matters more here than sunburn does. It is the symmetrical brown patching across the cheeks, forehead and upper lip that is common in Fitzpatrick III and IV skin, worsens with sun and heat, and recurs stubbornly.

Singapore has its own trial. At the National Skin Centre, 40 patients with Fitzpatrick III–IV skin were randomised to 480 mg of oral extract twice daily or placebo for twelve weeks, with everyone also using 4% hydroquinone nightly and SPF 50+ daily.8 Among the 33 evaluated, severity scores fell by 54.9% on the extract and 44.4% on placebo. The gap between the two groups was statistically significant at day 56, when the extract group had improved 49.4% against the placebo group's 32.6%.

Read that honestly and it says something specific: patients on the extract improved faster, and by three months both groups had improved substantially. This was a small pilot, it was sponsored by the manufacturer, and two of its authors were employees of that manufacturer.8 The broader 2025 review records melasma results across the literature as mixed, with at least one study finding no significant benefit over placebo.4

What the capsule is not, on this evidence, is a melasma treatment. What it may be is something that gets an existing treatment working sooner.

The line the category is not allowed to cross

In May 2018 the United States Food and Drug Administration issued warning letters to four companies marketing dietary supplements with sun-protection claims: GliSODin Skin Nutrients, Napa Valley Bioscience, Pharmacy Direct and Sunergized.9 The agency's position was that a product claiming to protect against ultraviolet damage is making a drug claim, which requires drug approval.

Heliocare was not among the companies that received those letters. But the episode set the boundary for the whole category, and it is worth knowing where it sits. Dermatologists commenting at the time drew the same distinction that the evidence supports: there is no such thing as an oral sunscreen, and a well-researched extract can still be a reasonable adjunct to photoprotection provided it is not sold as a replacement for it.9

The practical rule

Nothing in the published evidence supports taking a capsule instead of applying sunscreen. Every trial described above gave participants topical sunscreen as well, usually SPF 50+ or SPF 100+. The capsule was always tested as an addition, never as a substitute, so an addition is the only thing it has been shown to be.

Meanwhile, the thing that is proven

It is easy to spend an article on the capsule and skip past the evidence for the cream, which is considerably stronger.

In the Nambour trial in Queensland, 1,621 adults were randomised to daily sunscreen or discretionary use and then followed for well over a decade. Invasive melanoma occurred in three participants in the daily-use group against eleven in the discretionary group, a hazard ratio of 0.27 with a confidence interval of 0.08 to 0.97.10 The result for melanoma overall did not quite reach statistical significance, which is worth stating rather than rounding away.

The same cohort produced the strongest photoageing finding available anywhere. Among 903 adults under 55 followed for four and a half years, those assigned to daily sunscreen showed 24% less skin ageing on blinded assessment than those using it at their discretion.11 No supplement has evidence approaching that.

And the most common reason people give for skipping daily application does not hold up. A review of experimental studies, field trials and 69 observational studies concluded that there is “little evidence that sunscreen decreases 25(OH)D concentration when used in real-life settings.”12 The authors do note a genuine gap: the randomised field trials used moderate-protection sunscreen of around SPF 16, and no trial has yet tested the high-SPF products people are now advised to use.

What this means in a country with no low season

Singapore sits close enough to the equator that the sun is near-overhead for most of the year. Daily maximum ultraviolet index readings average six to nine, peaking between 10am and 3pm, and readings of 12 to 15 have been recorded during clear spells.1 There is no season during which the exposure argument goes quiet.

The local risk profile is also not the one imported from Britain or Australia. Analysis of 12,692 skin cancers recorded by the Singapore Cancer Registry between 1968 and 2016 found melanoma accounted for 5.8% of cases, with melanoma and squamous cell carcinoma rates remaining low and stable across all three major ethnic groups. Basal cell carcinoma, the least dangerous of the three, was the one that rose.13

What that leaves as the realistic daily concern is pigmentation and photoageing rather than cancer, which is precisely where the sunscreen evidence is strongest and where the oral evidence, such as it is, also sits.

So, is it worth taking?

A defensible reading of the evidence looks something like this.

  • It is not a substitute. Nothing in the literature supports replacing topical sunscreen, and every trial included it.
  • It is best supported as an adjunct in specific situations: alongside treatment for melasma, alongside sunscreen in people with significant photodamage or actinic keratoses, and in people whose skin reacts abnormally to sunlight, where the evidence is real but uncontrolled.14
  • The formulation matters and the research does not transfer between different extracts.5
  • The safety record is reassuring across the studies conducted so far, with no serious adverse effects reported.54
  • The trials are small and often sponsor-funded, so the appropriate confidence is moderate rather than high.

If you are choosing between buying the capsule and applying sunscreen properly every morning, the evidence is not close. Apply the sunscreen. If you are already doing that, are being treated for pigmentation or photodamage, and want to add something with a plausible mechanism and a decade of small positive trials behind it, the capsule is a reasonable conversation to have with your dermatologist.

What we still do not know

Three questions remain genuinely open. Whether oral photoprotection reduces skin cancer incidence has never been tested in an adequately powered independent trial, and the one attempt was withdrawn before enrolment.7 Optimal dosing has not been established, with published regimens ranging from 240 mg to 1,200 mg daily.4 And the effects on visible light and infrared radiation, which are described mechanistically in the manufacturer's own materials, rest largely on laboratory work rather than the human clinical endpoints that carry the ultraviolet claims.2

Those gaps are not a reason to dismiss the ingredient. They are the reason to describe it accurately, and to keep applying the cream.
